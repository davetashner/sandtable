# Lesson spine — 1914: a timetable the world fell behind

The narrative spine and learning objectives for the first scenario pack
(`content/eras/1914-schlieffen-marne`). Every beat, battle, widget and
decision point in Phase 1 must serve one of the questions below; anything
that serves none is cut. Story: `sand-1l0.17`. Review it before the content
stories (`sand-1l0.1` onward) start; change it by PR, not by drift.

## Thesis

Germany went to war in 1914 with a plan that was a **timetable**: beat France
in about six weeks by a sweep through Belgium, then turn east before Russia
was ready. The timetable assumed things the world did not supply — intact
railways, an undistracted right wing, a French army that attacked into the
hinge and stayed there, roads and feet that could keep the pace of the plan.
The campaign is the story of a plan falling behind its own clock, day by day,
until on the Marne the gap between the timetable and the ground became a gap
between two armies. The lesson is not "a better general would have won"; it
is that **a plan is a bet about time**, and the 1914 bet was lost on
logistics, distance and the enemy's choices as much as on any decision at
Kluck's headquarters.

One line for the header: _a timetable the world fell behind_.

## The six questions

Each question has a **claim** the beats make, the **evidence** the pack shows,
and the **surfaces** that carry it. Historiography is written in, not around.

### Q1. Why did Germany believe it needed a quick victory?

- **Claim:** the two-front problem plus Russia's slower mobilization made a
  short war in the West the only war the General Staff thought it could win.
- **Evidence:** the mobilization timetables (days to deploy, West vs. East);
  the 1905 memorandum's premise; Moltke's 1914 deployment splitting seven
  armies West, one East.
- **Surfaces:** prologue chapter (`sand-1l0.3`); the Russian-mobilization
  clock beside the campaign clock (`sand-1l0.18`); tech card _railways and
  mobilization timetables_ (`sand-w9t.2`); causal link July Crisis → plan
  (`sand-1l0.23`).
- **Historiography:** whether the "Schlieffen Plan" was an operational plan or
  a post-war construction (Zuber vs. Mombauer/Holmes/Foley) — stated in the
  prologue and the branch panel, never resolved for the learner.

### Q2. Why through Belgium?

- **Claim:** the French fortress line Verdun–Toul–Épinal–Belfort closed the
  direct road; the open north offered room for an envelopment in the
  Cannae tradition — at the price of Belgian neutrality and Britain.
- **Evidence:** the fortress line on the map (places layer, fortress rings);
  the 1870 Sedan encirclement as the model (thread `road-to-1914`); the
  ultimatum to Belgium and its reply (document card).
- **Surfaces:** prologue beat; places/forts layer; document card (ultimatum,
  `sand-1l0.25`); causal link Sedan → Schlieffen (cross-era).
- **Historiography:** how far Schlieffen himself expected British
  intervention and how much Moltke discounted it.

### Q3. What did the plan assume?

- **Claim:** ~39–40 days to decision, intact railways and bridges, a right
  wing at full strength, and a French army that attacked into the Lorraine
  hinge and stayed engaged there.
- **Evidence:** the plan clock vs. reality gauge; the Belgian demolitions and
  the Liège delay; Plan XVII's Lorraine offensive and its collapse; the
  "rail vs. feet" gap between railheads and marching armies.
- **Surfaces:** two clocks widget (`sand-1l0.18`); Liège zoom-in
  (`sand-1l0.4`); Lorraine zoom-in (`sand-1l0.5`); rail-vs-feet layer
  (`sand-1l0.21`); tech card _heavy siege artillery_ (`sand-w9t.4`).

### Q4. Where did the right wing bleed strength?

- **Claim:** Antwerp, Maubeuge, Givet and East Prussia each took corps from
  the wheel; by the Marne the outer wing was two corps weaker than the plan
  and marching on its own supply.
- **Evidence:** the running tally of detached corps; the two corps entrained
  east on 25–26 August; the masking forces at Antwerp and Maubeuge.
- **Surfaces:** right-wing-bleeds tally and force-ratio widget (`sand-1l0.19`);
  Belgium beyond Liège (`sand-1l0.20`); eastern cross-reference to Tannenberg
  (`sand-1l0.13`); the branch feasibility checklist (condition 1).

### Q5. Was it ever feasible?

- **Claim:** this is the contested centre of the lesson. The concept branch
  shows the sweep; the feasibility checklist shows what would have had to be
  true; the historians disagree and the learner sees the disagreement.
- **Evidence:** march rates vs. distance; supply beyond the railheads;
  Joffre's redeployment west by rail; the thinned wing.
- **Surfaces:** the Schlieffen's-concept branch with its checklist and
  historiography (shipped, `sand-a55.13`); counterfactual branches
  (`sand-1l0.11`); rail-vs-feet (`sand-1l0.21`); decision points
  (`sand-1l0.22`).
- **Historiography:** Zuber, Mombauer, Holmes, Foley, Herwig — named in the
  branch panel; the "success" branch is labelled hypothetical everywhere.

### Q6. What decided the Marne?

- **Claim:** four things in sequence — Kluck's wheel inside Paris, the gap it
  opened when he turned back to face Maunoury, Joffre's redeployment that put
  an army at Paris to strike, and the Hentsch mission that turned a crisis
  into a retreat — none sufficient alone.
- **Evidence:** the Ourcq, the gap, the BEF and 5th Army advance, Saint-Gond,
  Hentsch's visits; the orders of 4–9 September.
- **Surfaces:** Marne zoom-in (`sand-1l0.10`, seeded in `sand-a55.14`);
  decision points 25 Aug / 30 Aug / 4 Sep / 8–9 Sep (`sand-1l0.22`);
  documents (Joffre's 6 Sep order, Hentsch's report, `sand-1l0.25`); causal
  links (shipped: wheel → Marne, Instruction générale 2 → Marne).

## Rules for every beat

1. Name the question it serves in the PR description; if none, cut it.
2. Dates, numbers and positions cite a Source (`docs/sources.md`); contested
   points name the historians and their positions.
3. A beat is 120–220 words; the first sentence works on its own at the glance
   depth.
4. Hypothetical content says so in its label and its first sentence.
5. Human scale is carried by the casualty layer and first-person vignettes
   (`sand-1l0.24`) — not by atrocity imagery (ADR 0007).
6. The learner is never told what to conclude about feasibility; they are
   shown the checklist and the debate.

## Learning objectives (what a learner can do afterwards)

- Explain why the German plan of 1914 was a bet on time, and name the three
  assumptions it rested on.
- Trace on the map why the sweep went through Belgium and where the French
  fortress line stood.
- Describe how the right wing lost strength between Liège and the Marne and
  why that mattered.
- Give the sequence of decisions and events that produced the Marne, and say
  which were choices and which were constraints.
- State the historians' disagreement about whether the plan was ever feasible,
  and explain what the "success" branch assumes.
