# Historiography notes — 1914 contested points written as debates

The contested points of the 1914 campaign, each as the debate it is: who
argues what, on what evidence, and how the pack presents it. Beats, cards,
branches and causal links that touch these points must name the positions
and cite them, and must not settle the question for the learner (rule 6 of
`docs/sources.md`; reviewer checklist in `docs/fact-check.md`). Story:
`sand-23b.3`. Works named here are in the registry or listed "to add" in
`docs/sources.md`.

## 1. Did "the Schlieffen Plan" exist as deployed in 1914?

- **Zuber** (_Inventing the Schlieffen Plan_, 2002; later articles): the
  December 1905 memorandum was a staff study, not an operational plan; the
  German army never had the forces it assumed; "the Schlieffen Plan" is a
  post-war construction by former General Staff officers to explain defeat
  and blame Moltke.
- **Mombauer** (2001), **Holmes** (_War in History_ exchange), **Foley**
  (_Alfred von Schlieffen's Military Writings_, 2003), **Herwig** (2009):
  Moltke inherited and modified a real deployment concept — the strong right
  wing through Belgium — and fought 1914 on it; the memorandum's ideas shaped
  staff rides, deployment plans and the August 1914 _Aufmarsch_ whether or not
  the word "plan" fits.
- **How the pack presents it:** the concept branch is labelled as an
  illustration of the memorandum's idea, its branch panel names both sides,
  the prologue chapter (`sand-1l0.3`) carries the debate; no beat says
  "the plan" without this caveat attached.

## 2. Was the western sweep ever feasible?

- **Against:** march rates and distances for the outer wing; supply beyond
  the railheads on a demolished Belgian network; the corps that went to
  Antwerp, Maubeuge and East Prussia; the French ability to redeploy by rail
  (Herwig; Strachan; the logistical critique going back to van Creveld,
  _Supplying War_, 1977).
- **For (or "not so clearly against"):** that a stronger right wing, fewer
  detachments and a Kluck who stayed west of Paris could have forced a
  decision before Joffre's counterstroke — the argument the 1920s General
  Staff made and that popular accounts repeat.
- **How the pack presents it:** the feasibility checklist on the concept
  branch (conditions, `met` in history or not, cited), the rail-vs-feet layer
  (`sand-1l0.21`), the right-wing-bleeds tally (`sand-1l0.19`). Lesson
  question Q5 is explicitly left open.

## 3. Moltke's culpability

- **The charge:** Moltke weakened the plan — strengthening the left wing,
  detaching corps east on 25–26 August, losing control of the armies from a
  distant headquarters, and then losing his nerve.
- **The defence:** the left-wing strengthening responded to a real French
  threat; the eastern detachment was taken when the decisive battle in the
  West seemed won and East Prussia seemed lost; communications, not
  character, put headquarters out of touch; and the plan's flaws were not
  his (Mombauer's study is the fullest treatment; Herwig is more critical of
  the 25 August decisions).
- **How the pack presents it:** the tally widget shows where the corps went
  and when; decision point 25 August presents the eastern detachment as a
  choice with the information available; beats name Mombauer and Herwig.

## 4. Hentsch's authority at the Marne

- **The charge** (Kluck, and the post-war German debate): a lieutenant-colonel
  ordered the retreat of two armies without authority, when the 1st Army was
  winning on the Ourcq.
- **The defence** (the 1917 Hentsch inquiry that cleared him; Herwig): Moltke
  gave him full powers (_Vollmacht_) to co-ordinate the right wing; Bülow
  had already decided to retreat when Hentsch reached him; the gap was real
  and the BEF was in it.
- **How the pack presents it:** decision point 8–9 September; the Hentsch
  report as a document card when its text is in hand (`sand-1l0.25`); the
  Marne zoom-in narrates both versions.

## 5. The "miracle of the Marne" framing

- **The framing:** a sudden deliverance — Gallieni's taxis, Joffre's calm,
  a German collapse of will.
- **The correction** (Herwig; Doughty, _Pyrrhic Victory_; the AFGG itself):
  a counter-offensive prepared from 25 August by a redeployment that used
  French railways as the Germans had used theirs, against an over-extended
  wing; the taxis moved a brigade and the story moved a nation.
- **How the pack presents it:** Instruction générale no 2 as the hinge; the
  rail-vs-feet layer; the taxis as a tech card episode (`sand-w9t.7`), not as
  the cause; the word "miracle" appears only in quotation.
- **The taxi count itself is a small debate inside the large one.** Sumpf's
  study gives 630 cabs and rather more than three thousand men of the 103rd
  and 104th Infantry; the AFGG and Herwig support the four-to-five thousand
  the pack's event card uses; figures of eleven hundred vehicles and six
  thousand men circulate in popular accounts. The motor-transport card states
  the range rather than a number, and says that no version of the arithmetic
  makes the taxis a cause.

## 6. Belgium, atrocities and the manifesto wars

- **Contested in 1914, settled since:** the German reprisals at Dinant,
  Louvain and elsewhere were real (Horne & Kramer, _German Atrocities 1914_,
  2001); the "franc-tireur" justification was largely imagined. The
  Manifesto of the 93 denied them; the Bryce Report exaggerated some of
  them.
- **How the pack presents it:** the Belgian chapter (`sand-1l0.20`) states
  what happened with Horne & Kramer as the source; the science card on the
  Manifesto notes the denial; no atrocity imagery (ADR 0007).

## 7. Did the 1919 eclipse actually confirm general relativity?

- **The popular version:** Eddington sailed to Príncipe, photographed the
  eclipse of 29 May 1919, measured the deflection, proved Einstein right, and
  the announcement of 6 November made him famous.
- **The charge** (Earman & Glymour, _HSPS_ 11:1, 1980): of the three plate
  sets, the Sobral astrographic plates gave 0.93″ — close to the Newtonian
  half-value — and were discarded; the two sets that were kept gave
  1.98″ ± 0.12″ and 1.61″ ± 0.30″ against a prediction of about 1.7″. On this
  reading a theory chose its own evidence, and the error bars did not support
  the confidence of the announcement.
- **The defence** (Kennefick, _No Shadow of a Doubt_, 2019; Gilmore &
  Tausch-Pebody, _Notes and Records_ 76:1, 2022): the astrographic plates were
  rejected on stated instrumental grounds — a focus shift — and the decision
  was Dyson's, not Eddington's, Dyson being the more sceptical of the two;
  modern re-reductions of the surviving plates support the published figures.
- **How the pack presents it:** the eclipse card gives the three numbers as
  published, names Earman & Glymour on one side and Kennefick and Gilmore &
  Tausch-Pebody on the other, and does not settle it. The card is explicit
  that what is _not_ in dispute is the reception, not the reduction.

## 8. Did Hilbert get to the field equations before Einstein?

- **The charge:** Hilbert submitted a generally covariant theory of gravitation
  at Göttingen on 20 November 1915, five days before Einstein read
  'Die Feldgleichungen der Gravitation' in Berlin.
- **The reply** (Corry, Renn & Stachel, _Science_ 278, 1997): the printer's
  proofs of Hilbert's paper — published only on 31 March 1916 — show that what
  he first submitted was neither generally covariant nor in possession of the
  explicit equations, and that the proofs were revised after Einstein's paper
  appeared. Winterberg (2004) and others dispute the reading of the proofs,
  which are damaged.
- **How the pack presents it:** the general-relativity card names the dispute,
  cites Corry, Renn & Stachel as one position, notes that the reading has been
  contested since, and goes no further than the point both sides accept — that
  the two men were corresponding through the autumn.

## 9. Clara Immerwahr's death, 2 May 1915

A 1915 point, reached from inside the 1914 pack because the tech layer's
chemistry card has to say what Fritz Haber was. It moves to the Western Front
1915–1918 pack (`sand-g80`) with the rest of the gas material.

- **The popular reading** (Morris Goran, 1947 and 1967; Gerit von Leitner,
  _Der Fall Clara Immerwahr_, 1993, and the journalism and drama that follow
  it): Clara Immerwahr, a chemist in her own right, killed herself in protest
  at her husband's direction of the chlorine release at Ypres ten days
  earlier.
- **The scholarly correction** (Friedrich & Hoffmann, _ZAAC_ 642, 2016, with
  Henning's companion piece in the same issue): neither Clara nor Fritz Haber
  left diaries or correspondence from which her motives could be
  reconstructed, no farewell letter survives, and the protest reading was
  supplied by later writers without sources. They place the death in a
  confluence — an unfulfilled life, the marriage, the deaths of her doctoral
  supervisor Richard Abegg and of Otto Sackur — rather than in a single
  cause.
- **How the pack presents it:** the chemistry card
  (`1914:tech-poison-gas-chemistry`) states both readings, names Friedrich and
  Hoffmann, and says in terms that the sequence is not in dispute and the
  causation is. No beat asserts the protest reading, and the card does not
  use her death as a moral about her husband.

## 10. What gas cost: two casualty totals, neither of them firm

- **The four-nation table** (L. F. Haber, _The Poisonous Cloud_, 1986, p. 243,
  as reprinted by Faith in _1914-1918-online_): about 186,000 gas casualties
  for Britain, 130,000 for France, 107,000 for Germany, 73,000 for the United
  States — some 496,000 — published with the warning that gas casualty data
  are inherently inaccurate and that for some nations only generalized
  estimates exist.
- **The all-belligerent total** (Fitzgerald, _AJPH_ 98:4, 2008, and the
  interwar literature it draws on): more than 1.3 million casualties and about
  90,000 deaths. The gap between the two is mostly Russia, whose figures are
  estimates that cannot be checked.
- **What both support:** the ratio inside the number. About 2.6 per cent of
  British chemical casualties died; of the American gas casualties about
  1,500 did. Gas produced a great many casualties and comparatively few
  deaths, and fewer of both against troops with respirators.
- **How the pack presents it:** the chemistry card gives both totals, says
  which armies each covers and why they differ, and draws the conclusion from
  the ratio rather than from either total. No single figure is offered for
  "gas casualties in the First World War".
