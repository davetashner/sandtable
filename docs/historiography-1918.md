# Historiography notes — 1918–1922 contested points

The contested points of the Russian Civil War, and where each one lives. The
same rules apply as to [`historiography-1914.md`](historiography-1914.md) and
[`historiography-1917.md`](historiography-1917.md): a contested point is written
as the debate it is, positions are named and cited, and nothing is settled for
the learner (rule 6 of [`sources.md`](sources.md); reviewer checklist in
[`fact-check.md`](fact-check.md)). Story: `sand-ekc.8`.

Under [ADR 0017](decisions/0017-historiography-cards.md) the **card is the
source of truth and this doc is the register**. Where a point has a card, the
entry below says so and does not repeat the positions. Where a point has no
card, the entry says why not.

## The constraint this pack is under

Better than 1917's, and still severe. **One large secondary work turned out to
be readable end to end**: W. H. Chamberlin's second volume (Macmillan, London,
1935), out of copyright and downloadable, with a narrative, an appendix of
translated documents and a chronological table. Four modern open-access items
could also be read in full — Holquist in _Kritika_ (2003), Werth's Sciences Po
encyclopedia entry (2008), and Sumpf's and Mawdsley's 1914-1918-online articles
(2014) — and all four are post-1991 and archive-informed.

**Every modern monograph is borrow-only from this environment**: Mawdsley's own
book, Kenez, Smele, Leggett, Ryan, Buldakov, the Black Book, Pipes, Figes and
Smith at page level. So the cards below are built on one pre-archival narrative
that could be quoted at page level and four post-archival short-form works that
could be quoted in their authors' own words, and the difference between those
two categories is marked wherever it matters.

The second rule this pack applied throughout is the one about numbers. Where the
figure a reader expects exists only in works that could not be opened — the
totals for the terror, the death toll of the 1921–22 famine — **the pack prints
nothing and says why**. A large number carrying a citation that does not support
it is worse than no number at all, because the citation makes it look checked.

## 1. Did the Reds win, or did the Whites lose? — **carried by a card**

`1918-russian-civil-war:historiography-why-the-whites-lost`

Four positions: the centre, the railways and a year's head start (Chamberlin);
the absence of any politics to win with (Sumpf); the peasantry choosing the
lesser evil (Figes); and the intervention never being on a scale that could
decide anything (Mawdsley).

**Sourcing status: three of four positions are quoted from texts that were
read.** Chamberlin is quoted at pp. 454–457 from the chapter "The Revolution in
Retrospect"; Sumpf and Mawdsley are quoted from their own open-access articles.
Figes's position is stated from the central argument of _A People's Tragedy_ and
is explicitly not quoted, because that book could not be opened at page level;
the card says so in the position itself as well as in `unread`.

**The gap the card names.** Jonathan Smele's argument — that the singular in
"the Russian Civil War" is the mistake, and that what happened between 1916 and
1926 was a plurality of wars with no single "Whites" to lose them — is a
genuinely live fifth position and is **named nowhere on the card**, because no
text of his could be reached. Peter Kenez, whose two volumes are the standard
statement of the political-failure case, is likewise absent; the position is
attributed to Sumpf, who states it in a sentence, rather than to the historian
who established it. Both absences are recorded in the card's `unread`.

Reached from: Orel, the Omsk coup, Makhno at Ekaterinoslav, the Committees of
the Poor, the Archangel landing, the AFSR, the Allied forces in North Russia,
the insurgent army, and Denikin, Kolchak and Makhno themselves. It is also the
card the pack's opening premise would have pointed at, and could not — see §4.

## 2. The terror: how much, by whom, and why — **carried by a card**

`1918-russian-civil-war:historiography-the-terror`

Four positions: terror theorised on one side and improvised on the others
(Werth); ideology as the font (Pipes, Malia and the Black Book, characterised
through Holquist); circumstances and the weight of the Russian past (Buldakov
and Figes, quoted through Holquist); and Holquist's own case that both schools
de-historicise.

**Sourcing status: two positions are quoted from their own authors and two are
reached through a third party, which is labelled on the card in the terms ADR
0017 requires.** Werth and Holquist were read in full. Pipes, Malia, Courtois
and Buldakov were not; Holquist's footnote 7 is where their assignment to the
"ideology" school comes from, and the card preserves a distinction his
summarisers usually collapse — that Pipes emphasises the culture of the Russian
revolutionary tradition rather than Marxism as such. Figes is quoted verbatim,
but through Holquist and from a page of the 1998 Penguin edition that this
project has not seen; the citation is therefore given as Holquist's, not as the
pack's.

**The card prints no total, and that is the finding.** The literature that does
the counting — Leggett, the Black Book, Ryan, and the post-Soviet Russian
archival counts (Volkov, Erlikhman, Zayats), whose published figures differ by
more than an order of magnitude — could not be opened. The card's `unread` field
sets out why the range is so wide, which is itself part of the argument: the
counts differ on who is being counted, on what constitutes a political killing
in a country where the state was requisitioning food at gunpoint, and on whether
White and Green killing belongs in the same ledger.

What the card **does** state, because two read sources support it: Werth's
periodised figure of ten to fifteen thousand executions in September and October
1918; his comparison with the tsarist regime's ninety-two years of death
sentences; the Tambov camps holding some fifty thousand people, most of them
women, the old and children, with a mortality of fifteen to twenty per cent a
month in the autumn of 1921; and approximately 150,000 Jewish victims of the
pogroms of 1918–22, given independently by Werth and by Sumpf.

Reached from: the Red Terror decree, the shooting of Lenin, the Tambov rising,
Kronstadt, the resolution document, Dzerzhinsky, Antonov, the Kronstadt chapter
and the epilogue.

## 3. Did Kronstadt cause the New Economic Policy? — **carried by a causal link**

`1918-russian-civil-war:link-kronstadt-to-nep`, at `confidence: "accelerated"`
in relation and `contested` in confidence, with the argument in its
`historiography` field: against causation, that the crisis of War Communism was
general by the winter of 1920–21 and the change of course was already under
discussion; for causation, that Lenin announced the retreat on 8 March with the
fortress under bombardment and that the sailors' eleventh demand and his
concession are the same proposal.

This is a `CausalLink` rather than a card because the dispute is genuinely about
whether this caused that, which is the shape a causal link has. It would become
a card if a future pass could put named holders on both sides from works it had
read; at present the positions are reconstructible but not attributable, and
ADR 0017's bar is attribution.

## 4. The purge of the officer corps, and 1941 — **two causal links, both marked**

`1918-russian-civil-war:link-military-specialists-to-the-purge` is filed at
`confidence: "contested"` for a stated reason: **only Whitewood's abstract could
be read**, and an abstract states a thesis without showing the evidence for it.
Whether he traces the perception of the Red Army's vulnerability to subversion
specifically to the military specialists of 1918, or to the 1920s, or to the
foreign-intelligence panics of the 1930s, this pack cannot say and does not. The
competing explanations of 1937 — a fabricated German provocation, a factional
settling of accounts, a fear of a Bonaparte — could not be read either.

`1918-russian-civil-war:link-purge-to-1941` is filed at `confidence: "low"` and
asserts the chain without asserting a proportion. Its `historiography` field
records that the strongest version of the claim was made after 1956 by officers
with an interest in making it, and that the army of June 1941 was simultaneously
doubling in size, re-equipping and redeploying into newly annexed territory.

## 5. Why the opening premise points where it does

A small note for the next author, because it is a constraint rather than a
choice. `pack.opening.claim` is restricted by the validator to a card kind that
can carry working — tech, science, document, decision, clock, tally, supply,
casualties, vignette, link or person — and **`historiography` is not among
them**. The premise of this pack ("what is left to fight over is a railway map")
therefore points at the causal link that shows a corps of 35,000 holding five
thousand miles of railway, rather than at the card that carries the argument
about who won.

That is a defensible outcome and possibly a deliberate one, but it is worth
recording as a question: a pack whose central claim is contested has no way to
open on the contest. Filed as an observation, not a defect.

## What would change all of this

Access, as in 1917 — but a smaller amount of it. Five specific books would move
three of these entries: Mawdsley (1987), Kenez (1971 and 1977), Smele (2015) and
Leggett (1981). Whitewood's article in a readable rendering would move the
fourth. None of that is a fact about the field; it is a fact about the authoring
pass, and it is a bead rather than a defect in the record.
