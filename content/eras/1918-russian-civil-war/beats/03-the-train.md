On the night of 7–8 August 1918 a train was put together in Moscow and sent
east. It carried a secretariat, a printing press, a telegraph station, a radio
station, an electric power plant, a library, a garage and a bath-house; it was
too heavy for one locomotive and needed two; and it had its own machine-gunners,
because the country it ran through was not safe.[^trotsky-1930] Over the next
two and a half years it made thirty-six journeys and covered, on the count of
the man who lived in it, more than a hundred and five thousand
kilometres.[^trotsky-1930]

It is the most useful object in this pack. In a war with no line, command is not
a matter of moving reserves along a front; it is a matter of going to the place
that is falling apart and being there. The train is what that looks like when
you build it.

[Trotsky](person:trotsky-leon) left Moscow not knowing that Kazan had already
fallen — the Czechs and the Volga anti-Bolsheviks took it on 6 August, and the
imperial gold reserve with it. What he found at [Sviyazhsk](place:sviyazhsk),
a small town on an island twenty-five kilometres away where the beaten Red
forces had stopped, was described afterwards by a man who became one of his
political enemies: "lack of confidence in their own strength, absence of
initiative, passivity in all work and absence of discipline from top to bottom.
The arrival of Trotzky brought a decisive change into the state of
affairs."[^chamberlin-1935-2]

Chamberlin calls Sviyazhsk the Valmy of the Russian Revolution, and the
comparison carries a number with it that is worth staring at. At the start of
the fighting there the Reds had three or four thousand men in action on the two
banks of the Volga and the Whites a little over two thousand. The Whites had one
aeroplane; the Reds had five or six. This is the battle that Chamberlin thinks
may have been the most decisive of the entire civil war.[^chamberlin-1935-2]

## An army out of nothing

What was being built that month was the Red Army, and two decisions made it
possible, both of them Trotsky's and both of them bitterly resented.

The first was conscription. "Voluntary recruitment is only a temporary
compromise to which we have had to resort in a critical period of complete
collapse of the old army," he told the first congress of military commissars in
June; "everyone knows that the army which we are now building on voluntary
principles is regarded by the Soviet Government as merely
provisional."[^trotsky-military-1]

The second was to hire the enemy's officers. Tens of thousands of former
imperial officers were taken into the Red Army as "military specialists", each
with a political commissar beside him and, frequently, with his family
effectively hostage. Trotsky's case for it was flat: "There is not one of you, I
am sure, who will say that our army can manage without specialist
commanders."[^trotsky-military-1] The party men who thought otherwise — a group
around Stalin at Tsaritsyn among them — lost the argument in 1918 and remembered
it. It is one of the longest fuses in this project: see
[the purge of the officer corps](1918-russian-civil-war:epilogue-event-military-purge).

The third instrument was terror inside his own army. At Sviyazhsk he issued the
order that commissars and commanders of regiments that ran would be shot, and
when a regiment of Petrograd workers took to their heels, seized a ship and
tried to sail away up the Volga, he had the ship surrounded, the commander and
the commissar shot, and every tenth soldier with them.[^chamberlin-1935-2]

Elsewhere that month: the Allies occupied Archangel on 2 August, a small British
column reached Baku on the 14th, and on the 30th Fanya Kaplan shot Lenin outside
a Moscow factory. The consequences of that last are the next beat.
