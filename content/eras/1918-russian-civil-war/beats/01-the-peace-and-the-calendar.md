The Bolsheviks took power on a promise of peace, and the only people offering
one were the Germans. Negotiations opened at
[Brest-Litovsk](place:brest-litovsk) in December 1917 with
[Trotsky](person:trotsky-leon) leading a delegation whose real instruction was
to talk for as long as possible, because what the party was actually waiting for
was a revolution in Germany. When the talking ran out he tried a formula nobody
had used before — neither war nor peace, no signature and no fighting — and
walked out on 10 February. On the 18th the German army started east again and
met nothing at all. That is what ended the argument inside the party: not the
terms, which everyone hated, but the discovery that there was no army left to
refuse them with.[^chamberlin-1935-2]

The treaty was signed on 3 March and ratified on the 15th. Article I says the
state of war has ceased. Article III says the territories west of a line agreed
by the powers "will no longer be under her sovereignty". Article V says Russia
will demobilise "without delay", including "those army units newly formed by her
present government" — a clause aimed at the Red Army that did not yet
exist.[^avalon-brest-litovsk-1918] What went was Poland, Lithuania, Courland,
Estonia, Livonia, Finland and Ukraine: about a third of the population of
European Russia, most of its coal and iron, and a great deal of its best grain
land.

Twelve days later the government left Petrograd for Moscow, because the capital
of the revolution was now a hundred and sixty kilometres from a German
frontier.[^chamberlin-1935-2] It has never gone back.

## The thirteen days

In the middle of all this the Council of People's Commissars did something
small and permanent. Russia had kept the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind
the West, which is why the February Revolution happened in March and the
October Revolution in November — the instrument the
[1917 pack](1917-russian-revolution:pack) is built around. On 24 January
(6 February) 1918 the Council resolved to stop:

> The first day after 31 January of this year is to be counted not as 1
> February but as 14 February; the second day as the 15th, and so
> on.[^dekrety-1]

Eight of the decree's ten clauses are arithmetic — what thirteen missing days do
to wages paid on the 15th and the 30th, to pensions, to loan interest, to the
maturity of a contract. Only the last one is interesting:

> Until 1 July of this year, after the number of each day by the new calendar,
> write in brackets the number by the calendar in force until
> now.[^dekrety-1]

The Soviet state's own answer to dual dating was to print both for five months
and then stop. **This pack does the same thing, on the same date.** Before
14 February 1918 every date here is written Russian-first — _23 February (O.S.)
/ 8 March (N.S.)_ — as the 1917 pack writes them. From 14 February 1918 there is
one calendar in Russia and dates are written plainly, with no suffix, because
adding one would imply a distinction that had stopped existing. Every instant
stored in the pack is Gregorian throughout, as it was in 1917: an absolute
instant cannot be ambiguous, and the Julian date was always a label rather than
a time.

The days 1 to 13 February 1918 did not happen in Russia. Nothing in this pack is
dated to them, and nothing can be.
