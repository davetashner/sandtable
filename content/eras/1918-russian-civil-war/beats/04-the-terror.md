On 5 September 1918, six days after Lenin was shot and the head of the Petrograd
Cheka was killed, the Council of People's Commissars put the terror into
writing:

> …that in the present situation the safeguarding of the rear by means of terror
> is necessary; … that it is necessary to safeguard the Soviet Republic from
> class enemies by isolating them in concentration camps; that all persons
> associated with White Guard organizations, plots and rebellions are liable to
> be shot; that it is necessary to publish the names of all those shot and the
> reasons for shooting them.[^soviethistory-msu]

Little of that was new except the writing. Chamberlin dates centralised mass
terror from the risings of early July — over four hundred people were shot at
Yaroslavl — and notes drily that the commissar for the interior's claim about
the "extraordinarily negligible numbers of repressions and mass shootings" up to
that point "is scarcely borne out by the facts".[^chamberlin-1935-2] What
changed on 5 September was that a state announced it, gave it an institution,
and required the results to be published.

Five days later the Red Army took Kazan back, and the war on the Volga turned.

## How many, and whose

The scale is disputed, the disputes are political, and this pack refuses to
print a total. The reasons are set out on the card
([the terror](1918-russian-civil-war:historiography-the-terror)), and they are
worth summarising here because the refusal is a finding rather than a shrug.

What can be said with a source is periodised and partial. Werth, working from
the post-1991 archives, puts ten to fifteen thousand executions in September and
October 1918 alone, and observes that in a matter of weeks the Cheka "carried
out two to three times as many executions as the Czarist regime had pronounced
death sentences over a 92-year period from 1825 to 1917".[^werth-2008] He is
equally clear that the killing was not one-sided and that the shapes were
different. Of Kolchak's territory: "Compared to the Red Terror, White Terror was
never established as a system. Mostly uncontrolled and perpetrated by 'warlords'
such as the Cossack Atamans Semenov or Annenkov, White Terror nevertheless
targeted specific groups: Socialist militants, suspected Bolshevik sympathizers,
Jews (assimilated to Bolsheviks), in legacy with Old Czarist regime
discriminatory repressions."[^werth-2008] Unsystematic is not the same as
undirected, and the second half of that sentence is the half a shorter quotation
loses. And the largest single category in the
whole war belongs to neither Red nor White: "approximately 150,000 Jewish
victims (125,000 in the Ukraine, 25,000 in Belarus) died between 1918 and
1922".[^werth-2008]

Hold that number loosely, though, and the reason is instructive. Sumpf gives what
looks like the same figure — "About 150,000 men, women and children were victims
of serial pogroms in 1918-1920, i.e. murders or extortion preceded by physical
and sexual assault"[^sumpf-2014] — and is counting something else: victims rather
than deaths, over three years rather than five, in a category that explicitly
includes extortion and assault. Two independent post-archival works reaching the
same round number for different quantities is a reason for caution, not a
corroboration.

What cannot be said is a total, because the works that do the counting could not
be opened from here and their published figures differ by more than an order of
magnitude — and they differ partly because they are counting different things.
Executions by the Cheka; those plus deaths in camps; those plus the suppression
of risings; those plus the famine. In a country where the state was taking food
at gunpoint, the line between a political killing and everything else is a
choice, and every total conceals the choice that was made.

The other argument — the one about *why* — is older and has better sources. It
runs, as Holquist maps it, between a school of circumstances that finds the
violence in Russia's own past and a school of ideology that finds it in what the
Bolsheviks believed; and his objection to both is that "the violence is either
timelessly Russian, or the product of an ideology that immediately and
inexorably unfolds into violence and terror".[^holquist-2003] His own answer
puts the Russian crisis inside a European one that had spent four years teaching
every state on the continent how to manage populations by force. What
distinguished the Bolsheviks, on that reading, was not the invention: it was
that they went on doing it after the emergency ended.[^holquist-2003]
