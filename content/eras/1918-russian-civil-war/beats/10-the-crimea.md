[Wrangel](person:wrangel-pyotr) is the interesting one, because he is the only
White commander who tried to answer the question. His land law of 1920 gave the
peasants the land they had already taken and told them the new government would
not take it back — which is what Denikin had refused to say for two years, and
by the time it was said the territory it could be said in was a peninsula.

He came out of the Crimea into Northern Tavria in June, held the open ground
through the summer while Poland occupied everyone's attention, and was driven
back on 2 November. On the 11th the Red Army stormed
[Perekop](place:perekop), the seven-kilometre isthmus that is the only way in
by land.[^chamberlin-1935-2]

There was nowhere behind it but the sea, and this is the one operation of the
White war that was planned, ordered and carried out exactly as intended. On
14 November the evacuation began from Sevastopol, Yalta, Feodosia and Kerch;
something like a hundred and forty-five thousand soldiers and civilians went out
in a hundred and twenty-six ships and were landed at
[Constantinople](place:constantinople). The emigration that produced the taxi
drivers of Paris and the officers who would spend the next twenty years
quarrelling in Belgrade starts on those quays.

_(The figures for the ships and the people are the ones usually given and come
from Wrangel's own staff. This pack could not check them against a source it was
able to read, and marks them as a claim rather than a count.)_

The regular civil war ends here. Chamberlin dates it from the movement of the
Czechoslovaks in May 1918 to the defeat of Wrangel in November 1920 — two and a
half years.[^chamberlin-1935-2] What was left was the war the government had not
noticed it was fighting, against its own population, and that one had four
months to run before it broke something.
