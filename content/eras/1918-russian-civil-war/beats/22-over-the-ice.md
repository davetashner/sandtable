Ten square kilometres of island, a fortress, two battleships frozen into the
harbour, and several miles of sea ice between it and the mainland. No routes
here either: the operation was two frontal assaults across that ice and then a
third that worked.

The Petrograd strikes came first, in the last week of February — a food crisis,
Menshevik leaflets calling for free factory committees and soviets, Socialist
Revolutionary leaflets calling for a Constituent Assembly, martial law, a curfew
after eleven, and cadet patrols in the streets. After several days the strikes
faded: "hunger made the workers apathetic; moreover, there was no leadership, no
clearcut programme".[^chamberlin-1935-2]

The [sailors](place:kronstadt) supplied both. Fifteen thousand of them met on
Anchor Square on 1 March, listened to the president of the Soviet Executive
Committee and the commissar of the Baltic Fleet tell them not to, and passed the
[Petropavlovsk resolution](1918-russian-civil-war:document-petropavlovsk)
anyway.[^avrich-1973] By the 2nd the fortress was in the hands of a
provisional revolutionary committee, and — a detail Chamberlin thinks worth
dwelling on — "for one of the most sanguinary episodes of the Russian civil war
Kronstadt is surprisingly free from bloodshed" at the start: the Communists on
the island were simply arrested, and every one of them survived the
rising.[^chamberlin-1935-2]

The government called it a White plot from the first day and never stopped.
It was not one, and the demands are the proof: no plot of generals asks for
freedom of the press for anarchists and left socialists.

The bombardment opened on the evening of 7 March. The first assault on the 8th
failed, and so did those of the 10th and the 12th; the Seventh Army in the
Petrograd region was in a "demobilization mood" and the attack had to be built
out of officer cadets, Cheka troops and Communist units, stiffened by three
hundred delegates sent up from the Party congress in Moscow. On the night of the
16th the assault parties were dressed in white robes and got almost to the outer
defences before they were seen. The fighting went on through the 17th. By the
morning of the 18th it was over, and the leaders who could get away went over
the ice to Finland.[^chamberlin-1935-2]

On 8 March, between the first assault and the second, the sailors broadcast a
greeting to the working women of the world for International Women's Day. It was
the fourth anniversary — by the calendar Russia had adopted three years earlier —
of the bread demonstrations in Petrograd that had started all of it.
