At five in the afternoon on 22 April the wind is right, and 168 tonnes of chlorine are released from cylinders dug into the German parapet north of Ypres.[^haber-1986] The cloud drifts over the French 87th Territorial and 45th Algerian divisions and they break, leaving a gap six kilometres wide with nothing behind it.

Nobody exploits it. The Germans have treated the gas as an experiment rather than an opportunity and have no reserves to push through; Canadian and British battalions close the flank overnight, urinating on handkerchiefs to breathe.

By the time the fighting stops a month later the salient has been pulled back to within three kilometres of Ypres — the only change 1915 makes to the line in Flanders that the map can show at all.[^1914-1918-online-ypres] The weapon stays. Within four months both sides have it.
