For thirty-five minutes on the morning of 10 March the British guns fire the heaviest bombardment yet attempted, on a frontage of two thousand yards, and the German front line ceases to exist. The infantry take the village of Neuve-Chapelle in under an hour.[^stevenson-2004]

Then it stops. The reserves are five kilometres back and the telephone wires forward are cut; by the time anyone can act on what the leading battalions have found, the Germans have brought up their own reserves and dug a new line behind the old one. Three days later the battle is called off, having gained about a kilometre.

Everything that will be argued about for the next three years is already here: a bombardment can destroy a trench, and destroying a trench is not the same as getting through. The year spends itself learning that four more times.
