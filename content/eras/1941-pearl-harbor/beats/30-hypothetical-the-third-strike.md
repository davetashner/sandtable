**This branch is hypothetical.** It did not happen, nothing on this map after the early afternoon of 7 December is a record of anything, and the pack draws its track as an approximate one for that reason.

In it, the [striking force](1941-pearl-harbor:kido-butai) does not turn away. It holds south through the afternoon, arms for shore targets, and flies a third strike against the things the first two waves did not touch: the fuel tank farms, the dry docks and machine shops, and the submarine base. On the argument's own terms the reasoning is clean. Battleships can be raised and patched; four million barrels of oil in unarmoured surface tanks cannot be replaced from anywhere nearer than California, and a fleet with no fuel and nowhere to repair has to go back two thousand miles and fight the next year of the war from there.

Now the objections, which are the reason the branch is here as an argument rather than as a conclusion.

**It was not in the plan.** The operation order says the force withdraws once the second wave is launched, and the only thing in it resembling a further attack is a contingency for repeated strikes if the destruction of enemy air strength goes favourably — air superiority, not installations.[^japanese-monograph-97]

**The men who would have flown it were not trained for it.** They had spent months rehearsing attacks on moored warships in shallow water. Nobody had briefed a tank farm.

**The story that somebody urged it is itself the disputed thing.** It comes down to us through [Fuchida](person:fuchida-mitsuo) and it changed in the telling: Jonathan Parshall shows that in the interview Gordon Prange took in 1948, Fuchida's priorities for a further strike ran damaged battleships first, ships in the navy yard second, and the *all important installations, oil tanks, etc.* third — which, in Parshall's words, *puts fuel tanks dead last on the list*. By 1963 Fuchida was telling Prange that on the flight back he *also had his mind on the destruction of U.S. fuel reserves*.[^parshall-2010]

**And the number everyone quotes cannot be checked here.** The four and a half or six million barrels that make this counterfactual sound decisive are not in any source this project could open for December 1941: the Navy's own construction history lists two above-ground tank farms and gives no capacity for them, and the six-million-barrel figure it does give belongs to the underground storage project, begun in December 1940 and not usable until September 1942.[^building-bases-1947]
