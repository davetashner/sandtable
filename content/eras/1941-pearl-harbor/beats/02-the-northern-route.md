The route was chosen because nobody used it. The [striking force](1941-pearl-harbor:kido-butai) went across the top of the ocean — up to 42° north, a long way above the direct line between Japan and Hawaii[^japanese-monograph-97] — and crossed it in the radio silence that the order's “utmost secrecy” meant in practice. What is up there in December is weather, and what is not up there is other shipping.

The price of that route was fuel and the price of fuel was oilers. A carrier can cross the Pacific; a destroyer cannot, and the [supply force](1941-pearl-harbor:supply-force) went with the fleet as far as the approaching point, refuelling on the way, with instructions to keep eight hundred nautical miles north of [Midway](place:midway).[^japanese-monograph-97] Refuelling in a North Pacific December, from a hose, between two ships that are both rolling, is the part of this operation that never appears in an account of it.

The order names two points in the empty ocean and this pack draws both of them. The **standby point** was to be reached by the evening of 3 December, at 42° north — and here the document contradicts itself, giving the longitude as 165° west in one place and 170° west in another. The pack takes 170°, draws the waypoint as an approximate position, and says so rather than choosing quietly.[^japanese-monograph-97] The **approaching point**, at 32° north and 157° west, is where the force would turn and run south.

Nothing on the map between those points is a position the force was observed in. The line joining them is a straight line and no ship sails one; what the pack knows is where the order said to be and when, and that the force was where the order said.

Meanwhile the other clock went on ticking. On 6 December [Roosevelt](person:roosevelt-franklin) wrote directly to the Emperor of Japan.[^peace-and-war-1943] By then the force had been eleven days at sea and was about a day from its launching position. There is no version of that letter, however it had been worded and however fast it had been carried, that could have reached anyone who could recall an operation running under radio silence.
