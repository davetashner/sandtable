At 0700 on X-1 Day — seven on the morning of 7 December in Tokyo, half past eleven on the morning of the 6th in Honolulu — the force turned onto course 180 and increased to about twenty-four knots.[^japanese-monograph-97] That is the moment the operation stops being a passage and becomes an attack, and it is the one leg of the crossing where this pack's own pace check raises a warning: the striking force was, on that leg, running well above the speed it had held for eleven days. The warning is correct and it is left standing.

Ahead of it, [Pearl Harbor](place:pearl-harbor) was doing what it did on a Saturday night. The battle line was moored along [Ford Island](place:ford-island), which is where a fleet lies when nobody expects torpedoes in forty feet of water. The war everybody in Hawaii was waiting for was going to start thousands of miles to the west, in the Philippines or Malaya or the Dutch islands with the oil in them, and every plan on [Kimmel](person:kimmel-husband)'s desk was about a Japanese move southward.[^morton-1962]

The American official history states the exception in one clause, and it is the most consequential clause in the campaign: *except for the richest prize, the three carriers and their escort, the entire Pacific Fleet was in port*.[^morton-1962] Where those three carriers were that morning, this pack does not say. It could not open a source that gives their positions, and it will not draw a track it cannot cite — so [the carriers](1941-pearl-harbor:us-carriers) are in the order of battle with no token on the map, which is an honest picture of what is known here.

The American account and the Japanese order agree on the arrival. The striking force *reached its launching position 200 miles north of Oahu exactly on schedule, at 0600 on the morning of 7 December*.[^morton-1953] The order says 230 nautical miles, at 0130 Tokyo time. The two accounts differ by thirty miles, and Morton's Tokyo hour is half an hour off his own Honolulu one — which is the arithmetic of Hawaii's 1941 offset rather than a disagreement about the operation. The pack follows the order. What matters is that a Japanese plan reconstructed in Tokyo in the late 1940s and an American official history written in Washington in the 1960s put the same ships in the same place at the same minute.
