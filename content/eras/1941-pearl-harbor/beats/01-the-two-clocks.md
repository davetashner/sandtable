At six in the morning on 26 November, Japan time, a fleet left an anchorage in the southern Kurils called [Hitokappu Bay](place:hitokappu-bay). Six aircraft carriers, two battleships, cruisers, destroyers, three submarines and a train of oilers, at twelve to fourteen knots, and — in the order's own words — with “utmost secrecy”.[^japanese-monograph-97] The bay had been chosen because there was nothing at it and nobody near it.

Later the same day, in [Washington](place:washington-dc), [Cordell Hull](person:hull-cordell) handed the Japanese ambassador a document called an [Outline of Proposed Basis for Agreement Between the United States and Japan](1941-pearl-harbor:document-american-outline): four principles, ten steps, and at step three the withdrawal of all Japanese military, naval, air and police forces from China and from Indochina.[^peace-and-war-1943]

Those two sentences are almost always written in the other order, with the note first and the sortie as its answer. **They cannot go in that order.** Six in the morning on the 26th in Tokyo is four o'clock on the afternoon of the 25th in Washington. [Nagumo](person:nagumo-chuichi) was at sea, on a westerly course out of the Kurils, before Hull sat down with [Nomura](person:nomura-kichisaburo).

That is the shape of this whole pack. There are two clocks running through the next twelve days and they are not measuring the same thing. One of them is a negotiation: a sequence of documents, each answering the last, in which both governments behave as though the outcome is still open. The other is an operation order — a list of positions with hours against them, which the force it was written for kept to almost exactly.[^japanese-monograph-97] The second clock was set before the first one finished, and nothing on the first clock was ever going to stop it.

The arithmetic between them is worth doing once, carefully, because the whole story turns on it and because it is easy to get wrong. In December 1941 Hawaii kept a standard time **ten and a half hours** behind Greenwich, not ten; Washington kept five; Tokyo was nine ahead. Every instant stored in this pack is Greenwich, and every time you are shown is named with the city whose clock it is. The two documents check each other: the operation order says the first wave launches at 0130 on X Day, Tokyo time, and the American air force history says the order to take off was given at 0600 in Honolulu. Those are the same moment, and only if Hawaii is ten and a half hours back.

Yamamoto's whole case for the operation was about time. He had told his own government that he could run wild in the Pacific for six months or a year and had no confidence in anything after that. A single blow at the outset was not a way of winning the war; it was a way of buying the months in which the war might be made unnecessary. Sixteen days after the ships left Hitokappu Bay, those months began.
