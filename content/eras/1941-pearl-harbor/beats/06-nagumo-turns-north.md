With the last aircraft back aboard, the [striking force](1941-pearl-harbor:kido-butai) turned north and increased speed. The order had said it would: *after the launching of the second attack units is completed, the task force will withdraw northward at a speed of about 24 knots*.[^japanese-monograph-97]

That sentence is the whole of the operational case for what [Nagumo](person:nagumo-chuichi) did, and it is usually left out of the argument about it. There was no third wave in the plan. What the plan allowed was repeated attacks if the destruction of enemy air strength went favourably — a contingency about air superiority, written for a force whose aircrew had been trained and briefed to sink ships.[^japanese-monograph-97] Nothing in it is about fuel tanks, dry docks or submarine pens, and nothing in the Imperial Navy's thirty years of doctrine had taught anybody aboard to think about them.

He also did not know where three American aircraft carriers were.[^morton-1962] They had been at sea that morning; they were still at sea that afternoon; and a carrier admiral who stays inside strike range of an enemy he cannot find, in order to attack shore installations he was not sent to attack, is running a different operation from the one he was given.

So the base survived the day it was attacked. The tank farms, the repair shops and the submarine pens were not touched, and [Pearl Harbor](place:pearl-harbor) went on being a fleet base two thousand miles west of California instead of a lagoon with wrecks in it.

Whether that was Nagumo's great mistake is the most argued-over question of 7 December, and this pack does not settle it. It gives you the [branch](1941-pearl-harbor:decision-1941-12-07-the-third-strike) to play, labelled hypothetical, and a [card](1941-pearl-harbor:historiography-the-third-wave) that names four positions and says which two of them this project could only reach at second hand. Read the card before you decide; the honest state of the argument is part of the argument.

The next day [Roosevelt](person:roosevelt-franklin) went to Congress, and a joint resolution declared that a state of war existed between the United States and the Empire of Japan.[^peace-and-war-1943] It had taken a little over twenty-four hours to turn a country that had spent two years arguing about whether to fight into one that was fighting, and the striking force was by then a day's steaming away to the north-west.
