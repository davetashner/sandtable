The radar saw them. At 0702 the set at [Opana Point](place:opana-point), on the ridge above the north shore, *plotted a group of airplanes at approximately 130 miles, bearing 0° to 3° east of north*. The plot went by telephone to the information centre about a quarter of an hour later.[^craven-cate-aaf-1]

The officer who took it was there, in the official history's own words, *solely for training and observation*. He knew that a flight of B-17s was due in from the mainland and that naval aircraft were out searching, and he *did not consider it necessary to take any action*.[^craven-cate-aaf-1] The set had done exactly what it was built to do, an hour ahead of time, and the answer to it was that nobody had yet built the habit of believing it.

Fifty-three minutes later, at 0755, single-engine aircraft were seen south-east of the [Hickam Field](place:hickam-field) hangar line heading for [Pearl Harbor](place:pearl-harbor), and the naval base and the airfield came under attack almost at the same moment.[^craven-cate-aaf-1] Two of the American official histories differ by five minutes about when the first bombs fell — one has the aircraft over Oahu at 0750 and the bombs five minutes later[^morton-1962] — and the pack takes the later reading.

It is worth holding both numbers in view at once. 0755 in Honolulu is 1325 in Washington, which means that at the moment the first bomb fell the Japanese ambassador had already put his one o'clock appointment back to a quarter to two and was not yet in the building. The aircraft in the air above [Battleship Row](place:battleship-row) had been launched at 0600 from a position the operation order named to the nautical mile.[^japanese-monograph-97] One of those two clocks had been running to a schedule for sixteen days. The other was being kept by two men who could not get a document typed.
