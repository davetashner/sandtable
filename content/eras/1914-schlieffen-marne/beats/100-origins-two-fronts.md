Germany's problem was not France. It was France and Russia at the same time, and it was made in Berlin.

For twenty years after 1871 Bismarck's whole diplomatic effort went into keeping the two apart: the League of the Three Emperors, the alliance with Austria-Hungary, and in 1887 the Reinsurance Treaty that kept a line open to St Petersburg. Wilhelm II dismissed him in March 1890 and let the treaty lapse. Within four years the thing the system existed to prevent had happened, and France and Russia were allies.[^strachan-2001]

The soldier who had won the wars of unification did not think this called for a bolder plan. From 1879 the elder Moltke's deployments were defensive in the west and offensive only in a limited way in the east; he expected a long war ended by negotiation, and in 1890 he told the Reichstag that a war among the great powers might last seven years, or thirty.[^zuber-2002] [^ritter-1958]

His successors reached the opposite conclusion from the same map. If Germany could not fight both at once, it would have to fight them one after the other — and fast.

[^strachan-2001]: On the lapse of the Reinsurance Treaty and the Franco-Russian alliance of 1892–94.

[^zuber-2002]: On German deployment plans between 1871 and 1890, when the eastern offensive was the German answer to a two-front war.

[^ritter-1958]: Ritter's account of the inheritance Schlieffen took over.
