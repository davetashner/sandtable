Schlieffen's successor was the nephew of the field marshal, and he spent eight years adjusting what he had been left.

Three changes matter. He spared the Netherlands: the sweep would go through Belgium alone, leaving Dutch neutrality intact as a 'windpipe' for German trade — which meant the whole right wing had to squeeze through the Liège gap, and Liège had to be taken at once.[^mombauer-2001] He strengthened the left in Lorraine rather than stripping it to the bone as the memorandum's arithmetic implied, because a French offensive there had to be met by something.[^herwig-2009] And he kept a reserve for East Prussia instead of accepting that the east must be abandoned for six weeks.[^strachan-2001]

The post-war General Staff turned that list into an indictment: a great plan, watered down. Since Zuber the charge has looked less certain — the ratios he is accused of ruining were never a fixed prescription, and the extra corps the memorandum wanted did not exist to be moved.[^zuber-2002]

What is not in doubt is that the man who took the army to war in 1914 had already decided the plan needed changing.
