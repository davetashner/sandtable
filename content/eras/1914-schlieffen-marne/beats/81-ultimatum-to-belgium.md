The plan the previous chapter describes required a road that ran through a
country Germany had promised to leave alone. On the evening of 2 August the
German minister in Brussels handed over a note giving twelve hours to reply. It
claimed France was about to march through Belgium, asked that German troops be
allowed to pass, and offered to guarantee the kingdom's possessions and
independence if they were.[^belgian-grey-book-1914] It ended by saying what
refusal would mean: Belgium would be treated as an enemy.

Belgium answered before the twelve hours were up. The note, the reply said,
proposed that Belgium sacrifice the honour of a nation and betray her duty to
Europe; the government would repel any attack by every means in its
power.[^belgian-grey-book-1914] It was a small country's answer to a great one,
and it was written in the certainty of being invaded.

German columns crossed the frontier on the morning of 4 August and made for the
[Meuse crossings at Liège](place:liege). That evening Britain's own ultimatum,
demanding respect for Belgian neutrality, expired unanswered.[^strachan-2001]
The timetable had begun.
