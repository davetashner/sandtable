Two things happened in 1915 that this pack's campaign clock cannot reach. One took a man out of physics. The other put the rest of the century into it.

Henry Moseley had already done his work. In two papers, in December 1913 and April 1914, he showed that the X-ray lines of the elements march in step with a whole number — not the atomic weight, but the charge on the nucleus, which is to say the element's place in the table and the reason there is a table at all.[^moseley-1913] [He took a commission in the Royal Engineers in the first weeks of the war](1914:science-moseley-enlists), went to Gallipoli as a signals officer, and on 10 August 1915 was shot through the head on the Sari Bair ridge above Suvla while telephoning a report back to divisional headquarters. He was twenty-seven.[^mhs-oxford-moseley]

Three months later, on four Thursdays in November — the 4th, the 11th, the 18th and the 25th — Einstein read papers to the Prussian Academy in Berlin, correcting himself each week in front of the same audience.[^pais-1982] The paper of 18 November accounted for the drift in Mercury's perihelion, some 43 seconds of arc a century that Newtonian gravity and the other planets could not explain, with nothing tuned to fit it.[^einstein-1915-perihel] The paper of 25 November was four pages long and gave the field equations of general relativity.[^einstein-1915-feldgleichungen]

That fourth Thursday was 25 November 1915: a year to the day after the last date on this pack's campaign clock, when the trench line reached the Swiss frontier and the war of movement in the West was over. Nothing connects the two dates except the calendar. Both are in it.
