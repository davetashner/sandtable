This chapter has been calling it 'the Schlieffen Plan'. Whether there was such a thing is the oldest live argument in this campaign's history, and the pack does not settle it.

Gerhard Ritter published the memorandum in 1956 and called the plan a myth of a different kind — brilliant, rigid, and beyond Germany's means.[^ritter-1958] Terence Zuber went much further from 1999: the memorandum was a staff study written to argue for more divisions, never an operational plan; the German army's actual deployment plans were more flexible; and 'the Schlieffen Plan' was assembled after the war by former staff officers who needed a masterpiece that Moltke had ruined.[^zuber-2002]

The replies came quickly and have not stopped. Terence Holmes argued that the memorandum describes a real operational intention and that Zuber's reading depends on discarding inconvenient evidence.[^holmes-2001] Robert Foley's edition of Schlieffen's own writings shows the same envelopment worked through year after year in staff rides.[^foley-2003] Annika Mombauer's study of Moltke finds him inheriting and modifying a genuine concept, not inventing one afterwards.[^mombauer-2001]

Nobody disputes what the armies did in August 1914. What is disputed is whether they were executing a plan, or improvising on an idea.
