[Joffre](person:joffre-joseph) orders the offensive resumed on the 23rd. Neither army can resume it. By the evening both are falling back on the Meuse and the Semois, and on the 24th the retreat is general.[^afgg-1-1]

The consequence travels sideways. The 4th Army's withdrawal uncovers the right of [Lanrezac](person:lanrezac-charles)'s 5th Army, which is at that moment fighting Bülow on the Sambre at Charleroi with Hausen's Saxons coming at its flank across the Meuse at Dinant — and Lanrezac, who can now be enveloped from two directions, orders the retreat that begins the Great Retreat. The Ardennes did not merely fail; it pulled the rest of the line back with it.[^herwig-2009]

For the pack's argument this is the day Plan XVII stops being an offensive and the campaign becomes what the German plan needed it to be — except in one respect that will matter more than anyone notices in August. The French armies are falling back, but they are falling back _intact_, in the direction of their own railways and their own fortresses, and Joffre is already beginning to move corps west behind them. Both plans have now failed. The difference is that the French failure leaves the French army on its own supply, and the German success leaves the German right wing marching further from its own every day.[^doughty-2005]

Joffre's remedy is to change the men. [Ruffey](person:ruffey-pierre) goes on 30 August, replaced by [Sarrail](person:sarrail-maurice) — one of some fifty French general officers relieved in these weeks, a purge that is either the ruthlessness that saved the army or the scapegoating of commanders for a doctrine the high command had written, depending on which historian is telling it.[^doughty-2005]
