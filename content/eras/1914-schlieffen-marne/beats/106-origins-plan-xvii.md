The French were not surprised by the idea of a German march through Belgium. They were warned, in detail, and they decided against believing it mattered.

In February 1911 Victor Michel, the commander-in-chief designate, proposed a deployment reaching north to Lille and Mézières, with reserve regiments paired to active ones to hold the extra frontage. The Conseil supérieur de la guerre rejected it; Michel was moved to the Paris governorship, and Joffre took his place.[^doughty-2005]

What replaced it drew on a doctrine already in the air. Colonel Louis de Grandmaison's lectures of 1911 argued that in an encounter the side which attacks first with everything imposes its will, and the 1913 regulations put the _offensive à outrance_ into the army's own rulebook.[^greenhalgh-2014]

Plan XVII, approved in 1913, is often called the mirror of the German plan. It was less than that: a concentration plan — where the armies would assemble — leaving the direction of the offensive to be chosen later.[^strachan-2001] But its centre of gravity was Lorraine, its assumption was that Germany would not put reserve corps in the front line, and both were wrong.[^herwig-2009]
