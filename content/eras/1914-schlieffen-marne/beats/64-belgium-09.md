Timed to the counter-offensive on the Marne, the field army comes out a second time on 9 September: the Demer and Dyle crossings are taken, Aarschot is retaken, and on the 10th the cavalry is at the edge of Louvain. The Germans turn the 6th Reserve Division, already marching south, back towards Brussels and bring up a division of the XV Corps from Alsace; by the 13th the army is back behind the forts. Edmonds is careful: none of this changed the Marne, whose retreat began on the 9th — it changed what reached the Aisne.[^edmonds-1925] [^strachan-2001]

So did Maubeuge. [Zwehl](person:zwehl-hans-von) marches on the 8th, by Laon, and covers some sixty kilometres in the last day and night to reach the Chemin des Dames on the morning of the 13th — in the gap between Kluck and Bülow, hours before Haig's I Corps climbs towards it. The corps the wheel had lacked on the Ourcq arrives one battle late, and the Aisne holds; the trench line begins where it stopped.[^herwig-2009] [^edmonds-1922]

Belgium's other legacy is outside the map. The accounts from Louvain and Dinant — the real ones, and the invented ones that came after them — fix 'the rape of Belgium' in neutral opinion, the United States above all, before the Marne is even fought; the German White Book of 1915 and the Manifesto of the 93 deny them, the Bryce Report the same year exaggerates them, and it took Horne and Kramer until 2001 to separate the record from both. The pack states what is established, cites them, and shows none of it.[^horne-kramer-2001]
