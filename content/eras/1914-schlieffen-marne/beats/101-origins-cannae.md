Alfred von Schlieffen ran the German General Staff from 1891 to 1906, and he spent it looking for one thing: a battle that would end a war in an afternoon.

His model was more than two thousand years old. At Cannae in 216 BC Hannibal let his centre give way, closed both wings on a Roman army twice his size, and destroyed it. Schlieffen worked the problem again and again in staff rides and war games — how to fix an enemy in front while the decisive weight came round a flank — and after his retirement wrote the studies that made _Cannae_ a word in the German army.[^foley-2003]

The attraction was not aesthetic. A battle of annihilation was the only kind of victory that solved Germany's arithmetic: an enemy merely beaten falls back and fights on, and Germany could not afford a war that went on while Russia arrived.[^ritter-1958] [^herwig-2009]

What Cannae did not supply was a way to make a modern army of millions perform it — or the extra corps such a manoeuvre would need. Both problems were left to his successor.[^zuber-2002]
