By 1914 the plan had become a railway timetable, and a timetable is a machine: it does one thing, and it cannot be told to do another at short notice.

The General Staff's railway section had scheduled some 11,000 trains to put the field army on the frontiers in seventeen days.[^strachan-2001] From that start the campaign was counted in mobilization days — Liège about M+12, Brussels M+19, the French frontier M+22, the decisive battle around M+39 or M+40 — and the whole schedule rested on an assumption about somebody else's railways: that Russia could not press the eastern frontier in strength for about six weeks.[^herwig-2009] Those two clocks run side by side under this pack's timeline.

What the machine removed was the option of stopping. On 1 August the Kaiser asked whether the deployment could be turned east against Russia alone; Moltke answered that a year's staff work could not be improvised in an evening.[^tuchman-1962] Whether that was true, or whether it was a staff officer defending his plan, is argued like everything else here.[^zuber-2002]

The elder Moltke had written that no plan survives the first encounter with the enemy's main body. In seventy-two hours the encounter begins.
