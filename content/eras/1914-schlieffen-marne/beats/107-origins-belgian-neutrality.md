Belgium's neutrality was not a Belgian idea. It was written for her by the powers, and Prussia was one of them.

The Treaty of London of 1839 made Belgium an independent and perpetually neutral state and put the great powers — Britain, France, Austria, Russia and Prussia — behind that neutrality as guarantors.[^strachan-2001] Belgium took it seriously in her own way: a small army, a policy of offending nobody, and a fortress programme at Liège and Namur designed by Brialmont to make the Meuse crossings expensive for whoever came, from whichever direction.

For Berlin the treaty was an obstacle to be priced. The memorandum of 1905 had already written the violation into the plan; what the General Staff weighed was not the promise but the chance that breaking it would bring Britain in — and Moltke's staff discounted that.[^herwig-2009] The Chancellor's reported remark to the British ambassador on 4 August, that Britain would go to war 'just for a scrap of paper', is the whole calculation in one phrase.[^hastings-2013]

The note demanding free passage, and Belgium's refusal, are in this chapter as documents. They were written seventy-five years after the guarantee, and twelve hours apart.
