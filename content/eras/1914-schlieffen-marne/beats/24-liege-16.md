Hollogne and Flémalle, the last two forts on the left bank, surrender on the morning of 16 August. The road and the railway through Liège are open; the 1st and 2nd Armies, which have been detraining on the Rhine and closing up behind the ring, begin their march on the 18th.[^herwig-2009]

Whether the twelve days cost the timetable anything is argued. The popular account — the 'heroic Belgian delay' — credits Liège with two or three days; Herwig points out that the armies were not due to march before the 18th in any case, and that what the fortress really cost was the coup de main's casualties and the railway, blown behind the retreating Belgians. What it gave the Allies was a story; what it gave the Germans was proof that the heavy howitzers worked — Namur, Maubeuge and Antwerp would learn the same lesson in turn.[^herwig-2009] [^strachan-2001]
