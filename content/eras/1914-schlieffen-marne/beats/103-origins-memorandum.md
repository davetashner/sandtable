In December 1905, in his last weeks in office, Schlieffen wrote out the problem and his answer to it. The document survives as a copy; the original was lost with the Prussian army archives in 1945.[^bsb-1000dokumente-schlieffen-1905]

Its argument is the one the map forces. A frontal attack fails; the promising line is from the north-west, against the flank and rear of the French position — and to get there the neutrality of Luxembourg, Belgium and the Netherlands must be violated. Everything then depends on the speed of the right wing.[^ritter-1958]

Two things about it surprise people who know only the legend. First, it names no dates: there is no forty-day schedule in it, and the timetable the 1914 campaign is measured against was staff work of later years.[^zuber-2002] Second, it says plainly that the forces required do not exist — that a war of attack consumes armies while the defender's grow, and that Germany would find itself too weak to continue in this direction.[^ehlert-epkenhans-gross-2006]

Schlieffen wrote the difficulty into his own answer. The document is one click away; read it before deciding what it was.
