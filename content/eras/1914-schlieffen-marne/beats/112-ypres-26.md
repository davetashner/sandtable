An army with no reserve, no depots and twenty kilometres of country left uses the only weapon the country still has: the sea it has spent eight centuries keeping out.

Behind Nieuwpoort, eight waterways meet at a lock complex the townspeople call the Ganzepoot, the goose's foot. It is still in Belgian hands, and two men know what its gates do because they work the polders for a living — [Karel Cogge](person:cogge-karel), water bailiff of the Noordwatering of Veurne, and [Hendrik Geeraert](person:geeraert-hendrik), a bargeman.[^van-pul-2006]

On the night of 26/27 October the old Veurne lock is opened with Cogge's guidance. The water spreads, and not far enough. On the nights of the 29th and 30th Geeraert works the overflow gates of the Noordvaart itself: open to the flood tide, shut to the ebb, so every tide leaves more behind than it takes. It is repeated until 2 November.[^van-pul-2006] [^belgian-army-report-1915]

The Germans take Ramscappelle on the 30th and are thrown out that night. By then the ground they crossed to reach it is going under.[^van-pul-2006] [^edmonds-1925] The northern end of the Western Front is decided, and the thing that decides it is a set of sluice gates.
