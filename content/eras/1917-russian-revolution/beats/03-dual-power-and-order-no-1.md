The arrangement that came out of the Tauride Palace in the first days of March is usually called dual power, and the phrase makes it sound tidier than it was. The Soviet had the crowds, the garrison and the railways; it had, in practice, the ability to stop anything happening in Russia. It did not want to govern. Its leaders were Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries who believed, on doctrine and on the evidence in front of them, that a backward peasant country had to pass through a bourgeois republic before anything else was possible, and that a workers' government in March 1917 would be swept away by the army and the countryside within a month.

So they handed the government to the men in the other wing, and kept the power to withdraw it. The Provisional Government had the ministries, the ambassadors and the letterhead. It did not have a single armed man who was obliged to obey it.

The document that made this explicit was written on the night of 1 March by a committee of soldiers in the Soviet, in a hall full of delegates shouting amendments, and it is the most consequential thing anyone wrote that year.[^wade-2017] Order No. 1 told the Petrograd garrison to elect committees in every unit; put the unit's weapons under the committee's control and out of the officers' reach; abolished the honorifics and the saluting off duty; and — the clause everything turned on — instructed the troops to obey the Duma's Military Commission only where its orders did not contradict the Soviet's.

Read it as what it was: a garrison, three days after mutinying, making sure its officers could not take the regiments back and hang the ringleaders. It was addressed to the Petrograd garrison and to nobody else.[^wade-2017]

It was read everywhere. Within a fortnight it was at the front, in an army whose discipline had been fraying since 1916 and which now had a piece of paper from the capital that appeared to say the soldiers were in charge of the guns.[^figes-1996] Whether that document destroyed the Russian army or merely described what was already happening to it is the oldest argument about 1917, and it was a political argument long before it was a historical one — the [causal link](1917-russian-revolution:link-order-no-1-to-the-offensive) carries both sides.

The [order itself](1917-russian-revolution:document-order-no-1) is worth reading before taking a side on it. It is four hundred words of housekeeping written by frightened men, and the sentence that mattered most - point four, on whose orders counted - reads like an administrative clarification.
