Three months after it took power the new government abolished the calendar that both revolutions are named for.

The Council of People's Commissars adopted the decree on 24 January (6 February) 1918 and Lenin signed it on the 26th. Its effect was arithmetical: the day after Wednesday 31 January 1918 was Thursday 14 February 1918. The first to the thirteenth of February 1918 did not occur. Nobody was born on them, nobody was married on them, nobody died on them, and no rent fell due.

The reason given was that Russia should count time as almost all civilised peoples counted it, and that is more or less the whole of it: the Julian calendar was thirteen days behind the sun and behind everybody Russia traded with, argued with and was at war with, and the church that had kept it there no longer had the standing to keep it there. It is the least bloody decree of the revolution and one of the few that has never been reversed.

What it leaves behind is the thing this pack is built around. Every date in the eight months you have just read has two forms. The strike of the women textile workers is 23 February and 8 March. The abdication is 2 March and 15 March. Lenin's train arrives on 3 April and on 16 April. The Winter Palace falls on the night of 25–26 October and on the night of 7–8 November. Neither date is wrong and neither is a translation of the other; they are two calendars over the same afternoon.

And the names outlived the calendar that made them. A revolution in March is still called February, and a revolution in November is still called October, in Russian and in English and in every history written since — by a state that had deleted the calendar in question inside a hundred days. The [card on the thirteen days](1917-russian-revolution:science-the-thirteen-days) has the arithmetic.
