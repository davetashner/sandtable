It began in the small hours of 25 October (O.S.) and it looked like nothing. Detachments of soldiers, sailors and Red Guards walked to the bridges, the central telegraph, the telephone exchange, the state bank, the power station and the four railway termini, told the guards they were relieved, and were. The trams ran. The shops opened. The theatres played that evening. Most of Petrograd learned that the government had fallen by reading about it.[^rabinowitch-1976]

[Kerensky](person:kerensky-alexander) left the [Winter Palace](place:winter-palace) by car at about eleven in the morning, with an American embassy flag on it, to find troops at the front. He got as far as Pskov and found none that would come.

The last building took until the small hours of the 26th, and it took that long mainly because the attackers were not certain how to attack it and the defenders were slowly going home. The Aurora fired a blank round at about twenty to ten as a signal. The Peter and Paul Fortress, whose guns were the plan's artillery, discovered on the night that they needed work, and eventually put two or three live rounds into the palace. Attackers had been walking in through unguarded side entrances for hours. At about ten past two the ministers were found in a first-floor dining room and arrested. Two men died on the government side.

There was no storming. The one everybody can picture — the great human wave over the railings, the crowd flooding the square — was filmed by Eisenstein for the tenth anniversary in 1927, and by some accounts injured more people than the original.[^figes-1996]

Down the road at [Smolny](place:smolny-institute) the Second All-Russian Congress of Soviets was in session while this went on. The Mensheviks and the Right SRs denounced what had been done in the Congress's name and walked out, which left the hall to the men who had done it.[^rabinowitch-1976] The Congress then passed the decree on peace and the decree on land and constituted a Council of People's Commissars.

The land decree is worth a sentence of its own, because it explains why the countryside did not rise. It was not a Bolshevik programme at all: it adopted, almost word for word, the Socialist Revolutionary policy that had been endorsed by hundreds of peasant assemblies through the summer. The peasants were already taking the land. The decree told them they could keep it.[^figes-1996]

Whether the whole of it was a revolution or a coup is the argument this pack refuses to settle for you. What can be said flatly is that the government of Russia fell in one night to a few thousand men, that hardly anybody defended it, and that the party which took it had been a discredited minority fourteen weeks earlier.
