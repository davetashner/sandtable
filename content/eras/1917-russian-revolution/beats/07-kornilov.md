By August the Provisional Government had no army it could rely on, no majority anywhere, and a Supreme Commander it had appointed precisely because he said he would restore discipline. [Kornilov](person:kornilov-lavr) was a Siberian Cossack officer with a public reputation and no political sense whatever, and around him gathered everyone in Russia who wanted the revolution stopped.

What happened between 22 and 27 August (O.S.) is genuinely disputed, and the pack is not going to pretend otherwise. Both men agreed that troops should be moved towards Petrograd; the [3rd Cavalry Corps](1917-russian-revolution:corps-cavalry-3) was put in motion with the government's knowledge. Then a former Procurator of the Holy Synod, V. N. Lvov, carried messages between the two in which each appears to have understood the other to be proposing something he was not. On the evening of 26 August [Kerensky](person:kerensky-alexander) concluded — over a Hughes teleprinter, at a distance of six hundred kilometres — that his Supreme Commander was demanding that he hand over power, and dismissed him. Kornilov, who believed the government had agreed to what he was doing, refused to go.

There are at least three readings in the literature. One is that Kerensky manufactured the rupture, and that its real cost was the destruction of what was left of the army's command.[^katkov-1980] A second is that the two men had a genuine understanding about using troops in the capital, and that Kerensky broke it once Lvov's report convinced him he was about to be displaced. A third is the Soviet account, in which the affair is straightforwardly a counter-revolutionary conspiracy of generals and property, and the misunderstanding is a story told afterwards by its authors.

This pack does not choose between them, and it also does not give the second and third a card of their own, because the works that hold them cannot be opened from here and a card that names a historian is a claim about what that historian argued. The register in `docs/historiography-1917.md` says what would be needed.

What is not in dispute is what stopped the corps, and it is the most remarkable thing in the whole episode. There was no battle. Railwaymen misrouted the trains, tore up track and sent echelons round in circles. Delegations from the Soviet — including, in the case of the Muslim horsemen of the Native Division, a delegation of Muslim notables sent up for the purpose — walked into the sidings and talked to the troops, who had been told they were going to Petrograd to defend the Soviet from a German plot and now discovered otherwise. The corps came apart train by train, strung out along the line between [Luga](place:luga) and [Vyritsa](place:vyritsa), sixty kilometres short of the city, and never reached the city.[^figes-1996] Its commander, General Krymov, went to Kerensky, was dismissed from the room, and shot himself the same day.

Kerensky won. It cost him everything. To meet the corps the government had armed the Petrograd workers and released the Bolsheviks it had gaoled in July; the rifles were not collected afterwards, and the men who carried them formed the Red Guards. Within three weeks the Bolsheviks had majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow Soviets, and the officer corps had concluded that the government would not stand behind it.[^rabinowitch-1976]

On 1 September Russia was declared a republic. Nobody very much noticed.
